Overview

Val's Vegan Kitchen needed a mobile inventory management experience that could support both restaurant managers and kitchen staff across multiple storage locations and food categories.

The goal was to make common tasks—such as updating quantities, preparing weekly orders, adding new ingredients, and reviewing inventory history—quick and understandable in a fast-paced restaurant environment.

Problem

Restaurant inventory work is repetitive and time-sensitive. Employees move through walk-in freezers, refrigerators, and dry-storage areas while counting items, while managers need to quickly identify shortages and prepare orders.

The app needed to help users:

  • update quantities after deliveries and weekly counts
  • identify low-stock items and calculate how much to order
  • add new ingredients and assign them to the correct location
  • review update history when inventory does not match
  • understand current quantity versus ideal quantity at a glance

Core User Workflows

I designed the project around four realistic restaurant scenarios.

Update a Delivery

Find an item, increase its quantity, save the change, and record the update.

Prepare an Order

Identify low-stock items and compare current quantities with ideal stock.

Add an Ingredient

Create a new item and assign its category, location, quantity, and unit.

Check History

Investigate when an item was last updated and who made the change.

Design Challenge

One of my biggest decisions was whether the inventory should be organized by food category or by storage location.

OPTION 1

Organize by Category

  • useful when reviewing similar foods together
  • helpful for managers preparing purchase orders
  • requires staff to move back and forth between storage areas
CHOSEN DEFAULT

Organize by Location

  • matches how staff physically conduct inventory counts
  • reduces unnecessary navigation
  • allows employees to finish one storage area at a time


I selected location as the default view because it better supports the real-world counting process. A sorting option still allows managers to switch to category view when needed.

Competitive Analysis

I reviewed Pantry Check, Your Food, KitchenPal, and SubVentory to understand how existing inventory products organize information, communicate stock levels, and support common inventory tasks.

What Worked

  • confirmation feedback after completing actions
  • simple quantity controls
  • history and timeline views
  • clear visual indicators for clickable rows

Opportunities

  • better support for location-based restaurant workflows
  • more visible low-stock information
  • clearer current-versus-ideal quantities
  • easier access to item details and update history

Initial Designs

The first prototype included a dashboard with low-stock alerts, an inventory page organized by location, and an Add Item form using dropdowns to reduce input errors.

User Testing

I conducted think-aloud usability testing with four participants. Each participant completed one restaurant scenario while I observed where they clicked, hesitated, became confused, and whether they could finish without assistance.

Key Findings

01

Low-stock alerts were easy to overlook.

A participant scanned the dashboard for several seconds and opened a category before noticing the alert area.

02

Users wanted confirmation after saving.

Participants were unsure whether a quantity update or newly added item had been successfully saved.

03

Inventory rows did not appear clickable.

A participant searched through the bottom navigation because they did not realize an inventory item opened a detailed page.

REDESIGN 01

Making Low-Stock Alerts More Visible

The original alert used a color palette similar to the rest of the dashboard, so it blended into the page. I changed it to a stronger red, increased spacing around it, and strengthened the visual hierarchy so urgent items receive immediate attention.

REDESIGN 02

Providing Clear Confirmation Feedback

The original workflow ended on the Add Item screen after the user tapped Save. I added a success screen that confirms the action and provides a direct path to the newly created item's details.

REDESIGN 03

Making Inventory Items Feel Clickable

I tested both pencil icons and chevrons. The pencil suggested editing, while the chevron clearly communicated navigation to another screen. I selected the chevron because participants immediately understood that more information was available.

Reflection

This project showed me how strongly information architecture affects the usability of a workflow. Organizing inventory by location was not simply a visual choice—it came directly from understanding how employees physically move through a restaurant.

User testing also reminded me that a design can appear clear to its creator while still hiding important interactions from other people. The low-stock alert, confirmation screen, History page, and chevron indicators were all shaped by moments where participants behaved differently from what I expected.

Future Directions

Although the prototype successfully addressed the primary usability issues, there are several opportunities for future improvement:

  • Barcode scanning for faster inventory updates.
  • Smart notifications for low stock and expiring items.
  • Recipe integration with automatic inventory deduction.
  • Role-based permissions for different staff members.
  • Analytics to support purchasing decisions.
